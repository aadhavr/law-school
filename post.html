should i go to law school

Published

October 26, 2026

I’m deciding whether to go to law school. My reason for going would be to become someone who makes good policy. That reason depends on three premises:

  • A. Effective policymakers are a bottleneck for solving world problems.
  • B. Scientists and economists are not the bottleneck. The answers exist but go unused.
  • C. Law school is a good way to become an effective policymaker.

Before running any model, I wrote down the tests and the decision rules in a pre-registration (PREREG.md). This post reports the parts I could run so far: Test A, and the “skill source” test, which is the first half of C. The results come first, exactly as pre-registered. After that come the places I departed from the plan, then the analyses I added later, which are labeled exploratory.

Short version. In Congress, some legislators really are more effective than others, year after year. That part of premise A holds up. But members with a law degree are not detectably more effective than members without one. Under my own pre-registered rule, the data gives no recommendation for law school. The analyses I ran after the fact add a caveat. The study can’t rule out a modest law-degree effect, so “no evidence” here is not evidence of no effect.

Data

  • Lawmaking effectiveness. The Center for Effective Lawmaking’s Legislative Effectiveness Scores (LES) measure how far each member’s bills get: introduced, acted on in committee, passed by the chamber, and signed into law. They weight bills by significance. I use LES 1.0 for the House and Senate (Congresses 93–118) and the state scores (SLES) for state legislators (1989–2024).
  • Background. Carnes’s CLASS data (version 1.9) records each member’s education and jobs, but only for Congresses 106–110. It covers 788 people. I matched 779 of them to LES by ICPSR number.

Pre-registered results

Test A: Is individual skill a distinct trait?

I fit a random-intercept model for each body separately:

LES_it = X_it·β + α_i + ε_it

X_it holds what comes with a member’s position: seniority, party, majority status and committee chair. α_i is what stays with the member from term to term. ε_it is what changes from term to term.

The test asks three things:

  1. How large is κ = σ²_α / (σ²_α + σ²_ε)? This is the share of the variance left after X_it that is stable within a member.
  2. What share of Var(LES_it) does X_it explain?
  3. Out of sample, does it hold up? For each Congress t, I estimate α̂_i using only earlier Congresses. Then I check how well α̂_i predicts the member’s LES residual at t (Pearson r).

All 95% confidence intervals come from a bootstrap that resamples members.

Decision rule (from PREREG, House is primary).

  • Supported: κ ≥ 0.20, and the 95% CI of r excludes 0.
  • Weak: 0.10 ≤ κ < 0.20, or the CI of r includes 0.
  • Not supported: κ < 0.10.

What this does and does not show. All three bodies pass the pre-registered rule. In the House, role and party explain 0.34 of the variance in LES, and the member explains 0.17. That shows legislators differ in a lasting way. It doesn’t show that effective legislators are scarce, or that more of them would solve problems. Premise A needs both, and the scarcity half isn’t tested here.

Skill source: Does a law degree predict skill?

I took α̂_i (each member’s estimated skill) from the House and Senate models above, which use every Congress. I then regressed it on background variables for the members in CLASS. Effects are in units of σ_α, the standard deviation of α_i:

  • House: σ̂_α = 0.600
  • Senate: σ̂_α = 0.427

The 95% CIs come from a bootstrap over members that refits the whole mixed model in every draw.

Which variable is the law degree? CLASS has law_ind. Carnes’s appendix describes “indicators for whether each member held a law degree, MBA, medical degree, master’s degree, or PhD”. In the data, law_ind = 1 for exactly the 1,153 rows that record a law school, and for no others. It is not the same as having worked as a lawyer:

  • 96 rows have the degree but no lawyer job.
  • 34 rows have a lawyer job but no degree on record.

What the pre-registered rule says

PREREG says the data supports “go to law school” only if all three of these hold:

  • κ ≥ 0.20. This is met.
  • The law-degree effect rule is met. This is not met.
  • Test C’s ratio r ≥ 2. This is still pending.

Because the second condition already fails, the data gives no recommendation for law school. Test C can’t change that. Under PREREG, the decision now rests on cost, time and reversibility, which this project doesn’t measure.

Test D: What does skill (α_i) contain?

Test D was added to PREREG as Amendment 1, after the Test A and skill-source results were seen and before any Test D data was downloaded. It does not enter the combining rule above. It asks whether α_i, the member effect from Test A, partly reflects things that can be measured in the previous Congress (t−1). Each predictor z is added to the Test A House model one at a time:

LES_it = X_it·β + γ·z_i,t−1/s_z + α_i + ε_it

s_z is the standard deviation of z on that fit’s own rows, so γ is in LES points per standard deviation of z. The 99% CI comes from a bootstrap over members (2,000 draws) that refits the model in every draw.

Rule (from PREREG). A predictor is supported if its 99% CI excludes 0 and |γ̂| ≥ 0.060 LES points (0.10 × the full-panel σ̂_α of 0.600). Otherwise the result is “no evidence”. The share of σ̂²_α explained is descriptive only, with no rule.

One limit follows from this table: (ii) has a ceiling at the threshold. If the true γ is exactly 0.060, γ̂ falls below 0.060 about half the time, whatever the sample size. So (ii) at γ = 0.060 can be at most 0.50. A true effect right at the threshold is a coin flip under this rule.

What the CIs show.

  • (a) and (d): effects as large as the threshold are excluded. The 99% CI for (a) (−0.052 to +0.024) and for (d) (−0.052 to +0.047) lies inside ±0.060 LES points. So these are not just “no evidence”: an effect of the size the rule calls meaningful is ruled out at this confidence level.
  • (c) is inconclusive. Its 99% CI includes 0 and reaches +0.091, above the threshold.
  • (b) is supported, but it is a sign of skill, not a cause. (b) is lagged cosponsor standing. Cosponsorship PageRank in t−1 is built from the same kind of bills that LES counts, and members whose bills draw cosponsors tend to have high LES. So (b) partly measures how LES persists from one Congress to the next. It marks skilled members; it does not explain where their skill comes from.
  • Most of α_i is unexplained. With (a), (b) and (c) together, about 83% of σ̂²_α remains unexplained.

For (d), the realized standard error (0.020) is well below the planned one (0.028). Part of the reason is that the (d) rows have a smaller σ̂²_α (0.209; σ̂²_ε = 1.187). With those components, the GLS figure is 0.024. That accounts for about 51% of the gap, not all of it. For (b), the realized standard error is 1.32 times the planned one, so (b) was less precise than planned.

A likely reason for the rest of the gap is that the variance is unequal across members. The GLS figures assume every member has the same σ²_α and σ²_ε, and the bootstrap does not. To check this, the cluster-robust standard error (CR1, clusters = members) was computed on the same fits. This check was also added after the results were seen. Before it was run, the rule was set that it supports the reason for a predictor if the cluster-robust value moves from the model-based GLS value toward the bootstrap value by at least half the gap. For (d), the cluster-robust value is 0.020 (model-based 0.024, bootstrap 0.020), which closes 101% of the gap. For (b), it is 0.024 (model-based 0.019), which closes 60%. So the check supports unequal variance across members as a reason for both. For (b), the cluster-robust value is still below the bootstrap value, so part of the (b) gap is not explained by this check.

Other limits.

  • (b) and (c) cover only t = 109–118. Bill data comes from govinfo BILLSTATUS, which this project used for Congresses 108–117 (GovTrack blocks automated access). (a) is judged on its full sample; the (a) row for t = 109–118 is for comparison only.

Bugs found and fixed before any γ was estimated.

  • Parser. All 72,206 BILLSTATUS files were parsed. 13 of them use older tag names for the bill type and number, and the first parser run skipped them. This was fixed before any predictor was built. 16 bills list no sponsor in the source.
  • (a) linkage. (a) was first linked to Voteview by the member’s LES-panel ICPSR number in t−1. A party switcher can have a different ICPSR there, so (a) was missing for 1 member-term (Jefferson Van Drew, t = 118). (a) is now linked by bioguide ID. If a person has two Voteview rows in t−1, the row whose ICPSR is in the LES panel is used, otherwise the row with more votes.
  • (d) linkage. (d) was linked by CLASS ID = LES-panel ICPSR. CLASS stores some party switchers under only one of their ICPSRs, so 21 member-terms of 4 people had no (d), and 9 people got the wrong first House Congress. (d) is now linked by bioguide ID, and the first House Congress is taken by person. Years in the state legislature changed for 2 people.

Implementation choices that PREREG does not specify.

  • Service in t−1 is matched by person (bioguide), because the LES panel gives party switchers a new ICPSR. α_i stays one effect per ICPSR, as in Test A.
  • Delegates and resident commissioners are not in the Voteview House roster for every Congress, so their cosponsorships are dropped from the network and their bills from (c).
  • For (c), each person has one party per Congress. A cosponsor with no party code in t−1 does not count as cross-party.
  • PageRank nodes are the whole House roster of t−1, so members with no edges still get a value. Mass from members with no outgoing edges is spread evenly. Self-edges are dropped.
    1. is missing, not 0, for a member with no first House Congress in Voteview.
  • s_z uses the sample standard deviation and is recomputed in every bootstrap draw.

Lockdown data: Do law-trained leaders lock down sooner?

not an exact replication. The replication below misses the pre-set tolerance on one term, so the law-trained result is reported next to the gap and should be read with it.

Wehner and Hallerberg (Journal of Politics) study how fast 168 countries ordered a nationwide stay-at-home measure during the COVID pandemic. Their replication files have a daily country panel (61,488 rows, 59 columns) and a leaders file (188 rows) that records each leader’s degree subject. PREREG asks for two things: first replicate their Table A5, column (1), then add a law-trained leader indicator.

Model. This is the specification in their Stata file, fit in Python (statsmodels PHReg). It is a Cox model of the days until the first stay-at-home order, with the time-varying COVID-death indicator and their regime, leader, health-minister, economic, political, religion and region controls. Ties use the Breslow method (Stata’s default), with model-based standard errors, no strata and no clustering. The sample has 19,974 country-days, 168 countries and 152 stay-at-home orders. No covariate is missing in the risk set, so 0 records are dropped.

The five religion dummies sum to one in every country, so together they duplicate the baseline hazard, and Stata drops one of them automatically. I drop the last one listed (Buddhist). Dropping a different one (Christian) gives the same log likelihood and the same non-religion hazard ratios (match: yes).

Replication check. The tolerance was set before the fit: each HR within 0.01 and each CI end within 0.02.

Deviations from the pre-registration

Each item is listed in full in ANALYSIS_LOG.md.

  1. A discarded first run. My first run of Test A added a chamber term to the state model and fit three extra models. None of these are in PREREG. I discarded that run before reporting anything; the files are in archive/discarded_run1/. Every result above uses the PREREG model exactly.
  2. Control variables. PREREG says “background variables” but doesn’t list them. I chose doctorate, female, Black and Hispanic.
  3. A rule I added after seeing the counts. A background variable enters a model only if at least 10 members hold it. I set this after seeing the counts but before seeing any coefficients. It drops doctorate, Black and Hispanic from the Senate model.
  4. Sample. The law-degree test can use only Congresses 106–110, because that is all CLASS covers.
  5. What counts as the degree. law_ind counts any law degree, not only the JD. CLASS’s doctorate flag also counts Ed.D. and Ed.S. degrees.
  6. Which σ_α. PREREG says effects are “in units of σ_α” but doesn’t say which σ_α. I added this after seeing the results. Every law effect is now reported in LES points, in units of the full-panel σ̂_α (0.600 in the House) and in units of the sample’s own σ̂_α. The PREREG threshold, and the power at it, use the full-panel σ̂_α.
  7. Not yet run. Test B (are expert consensus answers used?) and Test C (which degree leads to policy roles?) aren’t done. Test B’s definitions are now fixed in PREREG Amendment 2, but no statement has been coded for adoption yet.
  8. Linking people, not ID numbers. The LES panel and CLASS use ICPSR numbers, and a party switcher can have two. The skill-source test above matched them by ICPSR, so three CLASS members were dropped and a few entered twice. The result above is unchanged as the pre-registered one. A re-run that links by person (bioguide ID) gives a House law-degree effect of +0.102σ_α (95% CI −0.014 to +0.218): no evidence for a JD skill premium. The Senate gives +0.116σ_α (−0.147 to +0.367): no evidence for a JD skill premium. Leaving out the one member whose CLASS records disagree on the law degree (Ralph Hall) gives +0.105σ_α in the House: no evidence for a JD skill premium. Test A run with one ID per person (19 House members have more than one ICPSR, covering 112 member-terms) gives a House κ of 0.253 (95% CI 0.201 to 0.328) and r = 0.408: supported as a measurable trait. Exploratory analyses E1, E2 and E4 below now link by person.
  9. Lockdown replication. The Table A5 replication misses the pre-set tolerance on the doctor-health-minister term. Under a fallback rule set after the first fit missed the tolerance, but before the final refit and before any law-trained model was fit, the law-trained leader estimate is still reported, labeled “not an exact replication”, next to the gap. One religion dummy is dropped for collinearity, as Stata does.

Exploratory analyses

Everything in this section is EXPLORATORY. None of it was in PREREG.md. I added these analyses after seeing the results above. They can’t change any verdict above, and they deserve less weight.

LES_it = X_it·β + γ·LAW_i + α_i + ε_it

  • House: 698 members, 5,838 member-terms.
  • Senate: 165 members, 1,528 member-terms.

The full-panel units are the ones the PREREG threshold uses. Here they divide by a fixed full-panel σ̂_α, so their CIs ignore uncertainty in that σ̂_α.   • Pre-registered estimate (House):
    • Bootstrap standard error: 0.060σ_α.
    • Smallest detectable effect: 0.169σ_α.
    • Power at the threshold: 0.91.
    • But that estimate is attenuated by the shrinkage in α̂_i, so a true 0.20σ_α effect would show up smaller than 0.20 on this scale.
  • Mixed-model estimate (E1, House):
    • Bootstrap standard error: 0.082σ_α.
    • Smallest detectable effect: 0.231σ_α.
    • Power at the threshold: 0.68.
  • Senate: power at the threshold is lower still: 0.31 for the pre-registered estimate and 0.21 for E1.

In plain terms: in the House, the less-biased mixed model has less than 80% power to detect a law-degree effect of the size PREREG called meaningful. The Senate has much less. “No evidence” here does not mean “no effect”.

E3: Does skew drive Test A? LES is right-skewed, so a few outlier members could inflate σ²_α. I refit the House model with LES replaced by its rank within each Congress, mapped to a normal scale (an inverse-normal rank transform).

E5: How rare are economics PhDs? 3 of 781 CLASS members hold one, or 0.38% (Wilson 95% CI 0.13% to 1.12%). Restricting to the 779 matched members gives 0.39% (0.13% to 1.13%). Even the upper bounds fall far short of the 30 holders needed for a test. Congress, at least, contains almost no economists to compare against lawyers.

Limits, in plain language

  • Selection. People who go to law school differ from people who don’t, before they ever enroll. So do people who win elections. These are comparisons among people who already made it into Congress. They say nothing about what law school would do for me.
  • α_i is not personal skill alone. It includes everything that stays with a member across terms: staff, donor and party networks, a safe seat, the district’s interests, and the reputation that draws co-sponsors. “Skill” here means “whatever makes this member’s bills advance, beyond their role and party.”
  • LES measures how far bills advance, not whether they are good policy. An effective legislator by this measure can pass bad laws.
  • Legislators are one kind of policymaker. Agency staff, judges, executives and advisers aren’t in these data. That’s a large gap for premise C, since many lawyers who shape policy never run for office.
  • Coverage. Education data covers only Congresses 106–110. I have no degree data for state legislators.
  • Missing data. 127 House rows (delegates without an ICPSR number) are dropped.
  • Scale. LES is scaled within each chamber and state, so the size of α_i can’t be compared across bodies, only κ and r.
